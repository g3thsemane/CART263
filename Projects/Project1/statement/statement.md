Artist Statement:
You Can Run And You Can Hide But You Can't Run And You Can't Hide

    The Project You Can Run And You Can Hide But You Can’t Run And You Can’t Hide is an interactive browser-based game that critiques the intrusive logic of digital advertising. In the game, the player controls a constantly moving object navigating through an increasingly dense field of pop-up windows. As the player progresses, the advertisements begin appearing more frequently, gradually overwhelming the environment and obstructing movement. The game ends when the player collides with a pop-up advertisement. Through this design, the project turns everyday contemporary internet navigation into a playable system. Rather than simply representing advertisements visually, the game turns them into hostile obstacles that shape the player’s environment and ultimately determine the conditions of survival.

    The conceptual foundation of the project is rooted in critiques of the economic structures underlying the modern internet. In his essay “The Internet’s Original Sin”, Ethan Zuckerman explains how the early web became dependent on advertising as its primary funding model.

This decision helped shape the design of digital spaces by introducing an incentive to build systems that hold and monetize user attention. Zuckerman recounts how pop-up advertisements were originally developed in the late 1990s as a technical solution. Advertisers wanted their content displayed separately from potentially controversial web pages, so developers opted for windows that appear independently from the main site. Although they were initially made as a solution, the pop-up format allowed for a new form of online advertising that disrupts the user experience rather than existing alongside it.

    Zuckerman describes this advertising-driven structure as the “original sin” of the web. By relying on advertising revenue websites became incentivized to maximize visibility and engagement rather than prioritizing the user experience. Over time, this model produced increasingly intrusive forms of advertising such as banners, pop-ups, autoplay videos, and algorithmically targeted content. You Can Run And You Can Hide But You Can’t Run And You Can’t Hide translates this historical internet issue into an interactive game. The player begins the game in an environment where advertisements appear occasionally and navigation remains manageable. However, as time progresses, the density of pop-ups increases until the screen becomes filled with obstacles. This escalation mirrors the evolution of online advertising from relatively tame and unobtrusive banners, to a form of pervasive bombardment seen on contemporary digital platforms.

    The project also engages with Richard Seymour’s analysis of digital platforms in “The Machine Always Wins”. Seymour argues that modern internet platforms operate within an attention economy that converts human focus into measurable and monetizable data. Social media platforms, advertising networks, and recommendation algorithms are designed to capture attention and hold it for as long as possible. Rather than functioning as neutral communication tools, these systems actively compete for the user’s engagement through continuous stimulation and feedback loops.

Within You Can Run And You Can Hide But You Can’t Run And You Can’t Hide, this competition for attention is represented through gameplay mechanics. Advertisements appear suddenly and unpredictably, demanding immediate response from the player. Each new pop-up interrupts the player’s path, forcing rapid changes in direction. As the spawn rate increases, the player must continuously react to new obstacles appearing across the screen. The resulting gameplay produces a sense of urgency and stress that reflects the cognitive overload seen when navigating ad-saturated digital environments. Eventually, the system becomes so dense that avoiding advertisements becomes nearly impossible, reinforcing Seymour’s argument that attention economies are structured in ways that ensure constant engagement within the system.

In addition to these critiques of advertising and attention economies, the project also draws on Lev Manovich’s analysis of digital media in “Database as a Genre of New Media”. Manovich argues that many digital environments function as databases composed of discrete elements that are dynamically assembled through software processes. Rather than presenting fixed sequences of information, digital systems generate content in real time by selecting and arranging data elements according to programmed rules. This concept is reflected in the structure of You Can Run And You Can Hide But You Can’t Run And You Can’t Hide. Advertisements within the game are not individually placed manually; instead, they are generated by an algorithm that determines when and where each pop-up appears. The game logic and environment therefore functions as a constantly updating system, mirroring the structure of contemporary advertising networks which dynamically deliver advertisements based on collected user data, grabbing attention. By modelling the design of the game after this system, the project highlights the algorithmic infrastructure present in much of the modern web.

The work also resonates with critical perspectives on data and technology articulated by Catherine D’Ignazio and Lauren F. Klein in Data Feminism. D’Ignazio and Klein argue that technological systems are never neutral, they reflect the economic and political priorities of those who design and operate them. Data infrastructures frequently reinforce existing power structures by privileging certain forms of knowledge, visibility, and participation while marginalizing others. Advertising driven platforms highlight this dynamic, their design prioritizes the collection of user attention and data, often at the expense of autonomy and transparency.

You Can Run And You Can Hide But You Can’t Run And You Can’t Hide aims to make these power dynamics visible by transforming advertisements into explicit obstacles within the game environment. In everyday browsing experiences, the systems that generate and deliver advertisements operate largely behind the scenes. Users may notice individual ads, but rarely encounter the actual mechanisms that determine their presence. The game removes this invisibility by placing the ads at the center of the experience, making them the primary force shaping the environment. The player is not merely exposed to ads, they must actively navigate around them, making the structural dominance of advertising impossible to ignore.

Formally, the project adopts a minimal visual style. The player is represented by a simple geometric object, while advertisements appear as rectangular blocks resembling browser windows. This aesthetic references the visual language of early web interfaces while emphasizing the structural nature of the work’s critique. By avoiding complex graphics or narrative elements, the game directs the player’s attention toward the interaction experience. The meaning of the work emerges through the player engaging with the navigation of an increasingly constrained environment. The spatial design of the game reinforces this conceptual framework. At the start of the game, the environment is quite open, allowing more free movement. The first wave of advertisements appear slowly and can be easily avoided. As the game progresses, however, the spawn rate increases, transforming the screen into a crowded and chaotic space. This progression mimics the historical transformation of the internet and webpages. Early web pages were relatively text-oriented, but modern interfaces are densely populated with advertisements, and other promotional content meant to grab the user’s attention.

Another key formal element is the player’s constant motion. Unlike many games where movement is optional, the player in You Can Run And You Can Hide But You Can’t Run And You Can’t Hide is always moving. This mechanic reflects the continuous flow of online information, where users are rarely stationary but instead constantly moving through streams of content through scrolling and algorithmic feeds. The inability to stop moving reinforces the sense that the player is trapped within a system that demands constant interaction. As the game becomes increasingly difficult, the player’s experience shifts from strategic navigation to frantic avoidance. The screen eventually fills with so many advertisements that escape becomes impossible. This inevitability is central to the project’s critique. In an advertising driven web landscape, avoidance is temporary. The system is structured to ensure that advertisements eventually capture the user’s attention.

Within the broader context of digital art and new media theory, You Can Run And You Can Hide But You Can’t Run And You Can’t Hide serves a form of actualized, procedural critique. Rather than presenting a textual argument about pop-up advertisements, the project places the user in a situation which makes an example out of the current systems engaged with. By going through the motions, the player’s experience of frustration and obstruction becomes a metaphor for the broader condition of the web. Ultimately, the project asks what it means to inhabit a digital environment shaped by advertising infrastructure. By turning advertisements into obstacles, the work exposes tensions between user agency and the economic systems that structure online spaces. The player’s inevitable collision with a pop-up advertisement mirrors the reality of capitalist web spaces. Within a system designed to capture attention, escape is always temporary.
